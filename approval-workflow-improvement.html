Approval Workflow Improvement

A project by Vimlesh Sonawane, Project Manager. Self-initiated DMAIC project on real public data (BPI Challenge 2020). Stakeholders were role-played; the fixes are proposed, not implemented.

What I created

I scoped and ran a DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control) project on a university's travel-expense approval process: a charter with three targets, a stakeholder map and RACI, a baseline from the real event log, as-is and to-be process maps, a root-cause analysis, a what-if model of three proposed fixes, and a two-wave roadmap and control plan backed by a RAID log and milestones.

What I analysed

The 2018 log covers 8,260 claims. The median paid claim took 8.05 days from submission to payment, the slowest 1 in 10 took more than 20.25 days (P90), and 13.14% of submitted claims (1,072 of 8,160) were rejected at least once. I found three bottlenecks:

  • Batched payment runs. 99.66% of payments were made on a Monday or Thursday, and the median wait for the payment run was 3.21 days, the longest of any step.
  • Long approval tails. The slowest 10% waited 4.85 days for the budget owner, 6.21 days for the supervisor and 6.83 days before the payment request.
  • Rework from rejections. Rejected claims took a median 13.21 days to pay, against 7.34 days for claims never rejected. 253 of the 1,072 rejected claims had a zero amount.

What-if model (estimates, not results)

Three proposed fixes: F1 daily payment runs, F2 a claim checklist with an amount > 0 rule, and F3 approval reminders after 3 days. Modelled together on the 2018 data (scenario S6):

  • P90 days to payment: 20.25 to 8.74 (target 14 or less)
  • Claims rejected at least once: 13.14% to 7.30% (target 8% or less). This is over the 7,907 claims left after zero-amount claims are blocked; it is 7.07% over all 8,160.
  • Wait for the payment run: 3.21 days to 1.00 day (target 1 day or less)

None of the fixes was implemented. The 40% cut in Administration rejections is an assumption; the zero-amount effect is observed in the data.

Significance

This project shows how I run process improvement as a project: agree the problem and targets with a sponsor, measure before proposing anything, separate what the data proves from what is a hypothesis, and hand over a phased roadmap with a control plan that names who checks each KPI, how often, and what triggers action.

Tools and methodologies used

DMAIC; process mapping; root-cause analysis (fishbone); impact/effort prioritisation; what-if modelling; RAID management; milestones and a two-wave roadmap; a control plan. Project tools: Smartsheet (schedule, RAID log, sponsor dashboard, roadmap, control plan), Asana (tasks, change requests, status updates), Miro (stakeholder map, RACI, process maps, fishbone), Tableau (baseline dashboard). Analysis: Python.

What is real and what is not

  • Real: the data, a published event log verified against the publisher's checksum.
  • Role-played: the stakeholders (sponsor, process owner, approvers, payments team). There was no access to the real organisation.
  • Proposed, not implemented: the fixes and the roadmap.
  • Estimates, not results: the scenarios are what-if models on the 2018 data.
