Agile SaaS Onboarding Rollout

New business customers waited 21 days before they could use the software they had paid for. I planned and structured a guided onboarding experience, delivered in three two-week sprints, with a proposed target of getting them ready in 10.

01The challenge

Maya has signed up. Why can’t she start?

When a business buys this kind of software, it can’t use it straight away. Four things have to happen first, and each one involves different people on both sides.

Until all four are done, the customer is paying for a product they can’t use.

Account set upHer company’s workspace and users created.
Data moved inHer existing records imported correctly.
Security approvedHer company’s security team signs off.
Team trainedHer people know how to use it.

Project initiation

I started with a project charter that set the scope (a guided onboarding flow for new business customers), what was out of scope, and the success measure: get customers ready for go-live in 10 days instead of 21.

02The problem

Why was Maya waiting 21 days?

Tap each stop on her journey to see where things got stuck.

?

21days in total, under the old process, before a customer like Maya could go live.

The problem wasn’t one slow task. It was a disconnected process with no clear owner.

See project details
Current-state analysis · stakeholder analysis

In Sprint 1 I mapped everyone involved in onboarding and reviewed the existing 21-day manual process. The five problems above came from that analysis: scattered checklists, email follow-ups, unclear ownership, late security review and no single progress view.

03The solution

One guided path, with an owner for every step

Instead of scattered documents and email chains, the proposed experience gives Maya one checklist to follow, says who acts at every step, and shows her what comes next.

Step 1Security checkCustomer + Team
Step 2Account set upTeam
Step 3Data importCustomer
Step 4Training handoverTeam
Step 5Go-live checklistCustomer + Team
One checklistEvery step in one guided flow, not spread across documents.
A named ownerEach step says whether the customer or the team acts.
Security firstThe review that used to delay launch now happens at the start.

Clear ownership removes the waiting between steps.

Requirements · backlog

I turned the solution into a product backlog of 5 epics and 10 user stories. Each story has acceptance criteria that define when it is done, for example a data import that shows clear success and error summaries.

The step order shown is an illustration of the proposed flow; the backlog and demo board in the repository hold the full detail.

    06Testing the solution

    What did a trial run reveal?

    Before launch, the flow was tested in a simulated pilot using 2–3 sample customer profiles. Their feedback led to two redesigns. Select a piece of feedback.

    Testing early changed the design before it could cost a launch.

    Pilot · feedback analysis · retrospective

    Sprint 3 ran the simulated pilot, analysed the feedback and produced two flow redesigns, then a training handoff checklist and a go-live checklist. A retrospective captured what to keep and improve.
